For most learners, memorizing vocabulary is the most exhausting part of picking
up a language. You review a list, and half of it is gone by the next morning.
The problem usually isn't your memory — it's the method.

Here are the techniques with the strongest research behind them, and that
actually hold up in daily practice.

## 1. Spaced repetition

Your brain stores information more firmly when you recall it right before you
would have forgotten it. Spaced repetition uses exactly that: you review a new
word after one day, then three days, then a week, then a month.

Reviewing the same word every single day is wasted effort. Get the timing right
and you learn roughly three times more in the same hours.

## 2. Active recall, not passive reading

Looking at a list and thinking "yes, I know this one" isn't learning — it's
**recognition**. The real test is different: can you produce the translation
without seeing it?

Testing yourself by writing is harder than reading. That difficulty is exactly
why it sticks.

## 3. Learn words in context, not in isolation

The word "take" has no single translation on its own. But learn `take a
shower`, `take a break`, `take care` and it falls into place.

Adding a short note about where a word is used cuts memorization time
significantly.

## 4. A little, every day

Instead of grinding 100 words once a week, do 10 words every day. Memory
consolidates during sleep, so spreading practice across days reduces the total
time you need.

Keeping a daily streak is also the strongest motivator there is.

## 5. Build your own list

Ready-made lists are a fine starting point, but the words that stay with you
will be the ones you noted from something you actually read or watched —
because you already met them in context.

## Summary

- Space your reviews; don't drill the same word daily
- Test by **writing**, not by looking
- Learn words inside sentences
- 10 words a day beats 100 a week
- Collect the words you personally run into
